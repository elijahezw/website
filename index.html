Calligraphic Chair Design and Fabrication
We set out to to build an outdoor lounge chair for reflecting, inspired by the earth’s topography. The product is a result of both manufacturability and organic aesthetics coming together to influence the form. Processes: CNC routing, milling, metal-lasercuting, tapping, tube bending, CAD/CAM
Processes: CNC routing, mill, metal lasercut, tapping, tube bender, CAD/CAM

Materials: Sapelle Mahogany, Aluminum

Timeframe: 20 weeks
Early prototypes used anthropometric data with scaled down models to design the form. These early prototypes also helped explore different fabrication process and their strengths.
I used CAD to quickly compare form and manufaturing choices and help aid decisions. I used FEA analysis to compare load cases on the frame and set up CAM operations for CNC routing of the wood.
I built three full scale profiles of form variants sing the intended joinery technique. This helped establish feasibility. For example we learned that with CNC joints we could create a varied profi le with interesting calligraphic shapes.
We compared a dozen wood species, ultimately sourcing an FSC Sapele Mahogany. Each board size had its own CNC routing CAM operation optimized to reduce waste. Finger joint operations were integrated into the cut file reducing our total operation count.
I measeure the linear spring back between die and output radius on our tube bender. I then built custom dies to precisely and reputedly bend the frame. Structurally, I added a rear support leg to prevent bending and a cross bar to prevent racking. I also tapped all holes for fasteners..
Tolerances become critical to this multi-part assemble. I designed laser cut brackets out of aluminum tubing with slots to counteract tolerance stack up. The laser cut the spacers and brackets are designed to all sit flush.
All 19 chair profiles are secured on a threaded 3/4in aluminum dowel with hex nuts. The large fasteners are then covered by the outer profile with security screw heads for peace of mind.
The chair was placed around Stanford campus over three weeks where I observed how people interacted with it.
The form of the chair was conceived in collaboration with teammates Aisha, Lucia and Alex. I designed the final build model, fabrication techniques and tooling processes. I built each of the 400+ parts directing extra hands for assembly.
Lamp 02 Design and Fabrication
This lamp aims to create aesthetic harmony between shape, size and materials. Using their placement and proportion their interaction with one another balance each other. Processes: Metal rolling, hydraulic stamping, brazing, turning, laser cutting.
Paper prototyping was critical to the form factor iterating through shapes and proportions. The overall balance was relative to each shapes interaction with one another.
The lamp post is made of turned brass, brazed with a rose tip torch to brass tubing. The lampshade is supported by a laser cut acrylic structure.
I cold cold joined the lampshade by press breaking interlocking tabs so that the sheet could hold its own shape. I further recessed the tabs so the surface is flush. Sheet metal technique bending required a clamp before press to stop the shape sliding.
Finally, I turned the brass button and a ring to secure it in place. The wood base has a naturally occurring crack which I filled with epoxy, then lightly sanded for a matt finish.

Wind Generator Design and Fabrication
I built this prototype for a functional horizontal axes wind turbine. The design features an 18 faced stator with iron cores. Processes: FDM, CAD Materials: PLA, ABS, Magnet Wire, Steel
I designed each part from scratch in CAD and 3D printed the entire assembly. I fit test to account for kerf and built in heat flow passaged. I also used catalogue components — fasteners, shafts, bearings.
Each copper wire spool was protected and placed on a steel bolt slotted into the plastic stator. Radially aligned magnets are place on the rotor. The wire is joined through a series of copper heat sunk terminals.
There were interesting challenges to the project. The coils need optimization between wire thickness and coil number to achieve a max power — this is because thin magnet wire has high resistance limiting current flow. The mounting systems needs to handle large bending moments from wind blades.
Spinning Brass Material Study
This study of metal spinning — a highly craft based technique — produced 40 different spun parts. I worked with are Aluminum 6061, 5052, 3003 and Brass. I used handheld tools and a roller. I built the tool holding adaptor and mandrels for the lathe.
There were many learnings in the process. The circumference of the workpiece must be kept in plane to prevent buckling. Aluminum 3001 and Brass spun best but require regular annealing to avoid fracture as the metal work hardens. Thicker metals work harden fast but are less likely to break if over stretched.

Steam Bending Material Study
For this chair study steamed oaks strips are fasted onto a plywood frame. This keeps the steam bent strips from returning to their original shape. It creates an imperfect and organic shape however its reproducibility is challenging.
I sourced the oak from a reclaimed wood yard and tested different steam chamber temperatures and timing. I found that steam bent wood had significant spring back that was hard to predict with grain variation and impurities.

Polarization Glass, Cellophane, Polarized Sheets
In this project I create three distinct compositions using cellophane viewed under a linear polarizer. This reveals dramatic color’s that change as the orientation of the polarize rotates.
When cellophane is placed at different angles different colors are seen through the polarizing filter. By overlapping these strips this compositions creates bursts of second order colors created by the mixing certain polarizations.
This composition is inspired by early constructivist works with their expressive circular and planer shapes. By overlapping multiple cellophane sheets in different orientations color polarizations are added and subtracted to create more colors.
The final composition has two polarization orientations which are reversed along the center line. This allows you to see the variation in color under different axes of polarization
The process behind “polage” is nuanced. Cellophane emits color frequencies in circular polarization. A linear polarizings sheet then filters out orthogonal frequencies. The orientation effects the frequencies seen and layered cellophane allows color mixing.
Albers Cardstock, Galeria Acrylic Paint
The perception of a pigment is relative to it’s surrounding. As you change a background hue, value or saturation, the accent swatch will be “pushed” perceptually in the other direction of that color space.

Exercise: Focus on the center line. How many aqua hues do you see?
The aqua swatches likely appear different hues although they are the same. Depending of the background, the swatch on green is pushed to be a blueish while the swatch on the blue is pushed to a greenish.
It’s not just your perception of hue that can be “pushed”. Colors can also be made to appear tinted or shade.

Exercise: Focus on the center line. How many purple tones do you see?
The purple swatches are identical. However, the dark purple background makes the small swatch seem lighter, while the lighter purple background makes the small swatch seem dark.
Saturation -- the same levels of dullness or vibrance of a color — is the hardest to manipulate.

Exercise: Focus on the center line. Are the small swatches in the center of each background the same vibrance?
Notice that the vibrant pink background should make the small swatch seem more dull while the duller background makes the small swatch seem more vibrant. In actuality they are identical swatches.
For the project I painted over 100 unique HSV combinations, then compared results to determine the best juxtapositions for the effect. The study is based on Joseph Albers work with students at Yale in his “Interaction of Color”.
Itten Dye-based Ink, LED strip, String
This exhibit aims to show how physical colors are relative to ambient light. 11 x 14 sheets of paper are arranged in a gradient from yellow to magenta. The ambient color in the room changes to effect the perceived color of the swatches.
The gradient is arranged to create a color tunnel. The colored paper reflects back colors present in the light of the room. Thus the color of the paper acts as a filter only reflecting certain wavelengths as the room changes color.
The project was executed over five weeks in collaboration with Lily and Ethan. Johannes Itten color studies on complementary cancelation from “The Elements of Color” were influential.
